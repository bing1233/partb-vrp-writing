# Part B VRP Writing

Make the manuscript understandable, its claims verifiable, and its mathematics consistent with its evidence. Learn transferable ways to assess and improve writing; do not encode one paper's transport setting, objectives, parameters, algorithm, or experimental findings as general rules.

## 1. Distinguish levels of guidance

| Level | Treatment | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Academic requirements | Always check; identify missing evidence rather than inventing it | Consistency of facts, equations, citations, data, and conclusions |
| Writing defaults | Choose according to the manuscript's purpose; adapt to explicit preferences | Section organization, table structure, proof placement, paragraph organization |
| Author and manuscript requirements | Distinguish reusable author preferences from requirements specific to one manuscript | Brighter figure colors are an author preference; notation, assumptions, and explanation structure depend on the manuscript |

Follow the user's current instructions. Do not present formatting habits as academic requirements or use preferences to justify fabricated evidence or conceal errors.

This package combines the author's writing requirements with [checked Part B examples](references/partb-evidence.md). The evidence record distinguishes visual inspection from text-only checks and author manuscripts from published versions. Do not infer a journal-wide style from a small sample. Current submission requirements have not been verified; consult official guidance when determining length, templates, article types, files, or submission rules.

When asked to learn from Part B papers before revising, inspect relevant original passages and figures first. Select VRP, pickup-and-delivery, or related routing papers that match the writing problem. If the existing examples do not fit, seek accessible full texts. Inspect images for colors and layout; abstracts, extracted text, and general plotting guides are insufficient. Record the paper, location, reading problem addressed, and conditions for transferring the approach. Never invent a default and describe it as a Part B convention.

## 2. Start with the necessary context

Read the latest manuscript specified by the user and directly relevant materials. Identify whether the task is planning, drafting, revision, review, or learning from reference papers. Do not substitute an older draft or conversation for the current manuscript.

Extract the requested scope, output language, research objects and decisions, established assumptions, actual method, available evidence, author preferences, and content that must remain unchanged. Do not ask again for information already supplied. Follow the requested output and manuscript language; the English instructions in this package do not require English responses.

For long or multi-turn tasks, use the [manuscript context template](assets/manuscript-context.template.md) in the manuscript project and fill only known fields. For a short passage, retain this information in working context without requiring a separate file. Never write a completed manuscript record back into this skill.

Handle missing information according to its effect:

- Directly correct language and organization when missing information does not affect the judgment.
- Preserve ambiguities that could change variable meanings, assumptions, algorithm order, or conclusions. Identify the specific clarification needed and continue independent work.
- Flag missing citations, data, or proofs in review notes or a working draft. Do not present placeholders as submission-ready text.
- When only part of a manuscript is available, state the reviewed scope; do not claim to have checked the whole paper.

## 4. Resolve each substantive issue

Work through **Locate → Assess → Revise → Recheck**, rather than merely listing principles.

1. **Locate:** Identify the sentence, equation, table, figure, or section. Explain where understanding breaks down or how a claim exceeds its evidence.
2. **Assess:** Distinguish a scientific-content issue from an argumentation or language issue. Give specific grounds and distinguish confirmed problems from questions requiring verification.
3. **Revise:** Provide replacement prose or edit the files within the authorized scope. Reorganize, clarify existing definitions, merge repeated arguments, or align claim strength with evidence. Do not conceal substantive gaps with polished language.
4. **Recheck:** Verify that the revision resolves the problem without introducing ambiguity. Check affected notation, cross-references, and other instances of the same issue.

Prioritize factual and mathematical consistency, claims and evidence, argument structure, then language and layout. Do not rewrite sound content simply to make the intervention look substantial.

If the user requests wording changes without additional experiments, use existing material to calibrate the argument. Do not make new experiments a prerequisite for delivering revisions. Identify unsupported claims while retaining contributions supported by the available evidence.

Searching the manuscript for similar problems does not authorize rewriting the entire manuscript. Inspect related locations, but keep actual changes within scope. For a review-only request, provide findings and recommendations without overwriting source files.

## 5. Deliver usable work

- **Planning:** Provide an argument suited to the research, the function of each section, and missing materials. Do not invent results to complete an outline.
- **Drafting or revision:** Deliver the text or edited files first, then briefly explain major changes and unresolved issues. Preserve the requested language, LaTeX commands, labels, citation keys, and terminology.
- **Review:** Prioritize issues that affect understanding or conclusions. Use location, problem, grounds, and proposed revision; do not manufacture one criticism per section.
- **Learning from references:** Give verified source locations, organizational functions, and transferable actions. Without the original text, report only what can be established.

Diagnostic tables are working tools, not mandatory reports. When the user requests brevity, retain the important findings and usable revisions. Keep internal rules, evidence records, and placeholder notes out of the manuscript itself.

## 6. Check before delivery

Within the revision scope and directly affected locations, verify that:

- The title, abstract, contributions, results, and conclusions share the same scope and evidential strength.
- Real-world facts, research assumptions, experimental settings, and personal preferences remain distinct.
- Definitions, equation indices, model explanations, pseudocode, and correctness conditions agree.
- Each experiment's question, comparison, metrics, and conclusion align; unfavorable results and unresolved instances remain visible.
- Citations support nearby claims, and statements about reference writing have an actual inspection record.
- Equation, figure, table, section, and literature references work; corresponding passages in bilingual manuscripts agree.

After editing files, check references and compile with available project tools where applicable. If tools or materials are unavailable, state the checked scope. Passing a format check does not verify mathematical correctness or paper quality.

## 7. Incorporate feedback

Classify new feedback as a transferable rule, an optional preference, or a manuscript-specific correction. Update this package only when the user requests a skill update; keep manuscript facts in the manuscript context. Retain cross-manuscript preferences only when explicitly requested, and label them as author preferences rather than journal rules. Each new rule should identify the signal, assessment, revision, and recheck. Update the inspection record when adding a reference example.
