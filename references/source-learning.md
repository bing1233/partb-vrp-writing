# Learning from Reference Papers and Sources

## S1 Do not turn impressions of a journal into rules

- **Signals**: Fixed sections, page counts, or formats are justified with “Part B usually does this”; a paper's writing is claimed as a model without checking the original.
- **Assess**: Identify whether a claim comes from official guidance, an inspected paper, an author preference, or this skill's writing advice. These sources are not interchangeable.
- **Revise**: Check current official guidance for submission requirements and record the page, inspection date, and applicable article type. Locate style observations in an actual paper, version, and passage. A small sample supports observations about those examples, not requirements for every Part B paper.
- **Recheck**: Each claimed requirement has an appropriate source. Do not infer that a submission must reproduce the layout of a published article.

Start with the [checked examples](partb-evidence.md), which distinguish bibliographic, textual, and visual checks. Coverage extends only to the recorded locations, not all VRP writing practices. When the user explicitly requests learning from Part B papers first, retrieve and inspect suitable full texts and figures before revising. Do not launch a large literature search for ordinary language editing when it was not requested.

Inspect rendered pages for colors, layout, and graphical structure. Extracted text can support observations about sections, definitions, and table content, but not claims about original colors. If images are unavailable, try legally accessible versions. If access still fails, record text evidence only; general plotting guidance cannot replace observations of the paper. Distinguish revision marks, hyperlink colors, and actual figure encodings in author manuscripts.

## S2 Learn organizational functions without importing the setting or argument

- **Signals**: Reading references leads only to copied headings, terms, or backgrounds; a paper's experimental settings become general rules.
- **Assess**: Identify the reading or reasoning problem a passage addresses: deriving a decision problem from difficulties, organizing a review by assumptions, explaining constraint groups, arranging theory and algorithms, or linking experimental questions to figures and tables.
- **Revise**: Record the original location, organizational function, transferable action, applicable conditions, and proposed revision location. Transfer relationships and explanatory methods using the current manuscript's facts. Do not copy distinctive wording, substantive arguments, or research settings.
- **Recheck**: The learned method remains useful after removing the reference paper's business-specific terms. The current manuscript's model and evidence have not been replaced by those of the reference.

Record the following in the manuscript project as needed; a separate document is not mandatory for every task.

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Source | Full title, authors, year, and a verifiable DOI or URL; do not invent missing details |
| Reading scope | Full text, selected sections, abstract, or screenshots; state access limits |
| Version and location | Author manuscript or published version; page, section, equation, figure, table, or passage |
| Observation | An original summary of the specific organization, without lengthy quotations |
| Assessment | Why it helps with the current writing problem and when it does not apply |
| Transfer | Where and how to revise the current manuscript, and which setting-specific details must not carry over |

When configuring reusable guidance, retain three source categories: a practice observed in a paper, a design judgment based on information structure, and an explicit user preference. A published practice may be rejected if unclear or unsuitable for the current manuscript; explain why. Learning from an example does not require copying it unconditionally.

## S3 Continue useful work when only an abstract is available

- **Signals**: Only a title, abstract, or bibliographic record is available, but the task requires comparing constraints, pseudocode, model explanations, or proof structure.
- **Assess**: An abstract can establish the research subject and the broad method or results explicitly reported. Omitting a detail from the abstract does not establish its absence from the paper. Bibliographic metadata verifies identity, not detailed claims.
- **Revise**: Use known information to organize provisional review directions and supportable prose. Mark comparisons requiring full-text verification and seek legally accessible full text when needed. If it cannot be read, state the limit without inventing writing practices or blocking other grounded revisions.
- **Recheck**: Statements such as “inspected,” “did not consider,” “outperforms,” or claims about an explanation's structure have sufficient evidence. Distinguish temporary placeholders from final manuscript text.
